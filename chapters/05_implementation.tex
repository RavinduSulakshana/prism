\chapter{Implementation}\label{ch:impl}

\section{Chapter overview}

This chapter explains how the artefact specified in \cref{ch:srs} was designed and built. It has four components: the Drone Trajectory eXtractor (\dtx{}), which turns the drone videos into trajectories, waiting times and calibration data; a Simulation of Urban MObility (\sumo{}) model of the junction; the Virtual LiDAR Sensor (\vls{}), which imitates an imperfect pole-mounted Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) unit; and the Pressure-based Real-time Intersection Signal Management (\prism{}) controller, the main contribution.
\Cref{sec:impl-dtx,sec:impl-sumo,sec:impl-vls,sec:impl-prism} present each algorithm and \cref{sec:impl-baselines} the baselines. \Cref{sec:impl-tech} justifies the technology stack and \cref{sec:impl-key} presents the key code.

The \sumo{} network, the \vls{} and \prism{} were first developed and tested against balanced demand split evenly across the four approaches; the drone-derived data were introduced afterwards, calibrating the model and parameterising the ten demand scenarios on which every reported experiment runs. The control law itself is purely mathematical; artificial intelligence is used only for
vehicle detection inside \dtx{}. \Cref{sec:impl-prism} also records how the control law was revised into \prism{}-R after its first evaluation, and why.

\section{Design of the algorithms}\label{sec:impl-design}

\subsection{Drone Trajectory eXtractor (DTX)}\label{sec:impl-dtx}

\dtx{} converts each one-hour 4K video (30 frames per second, processed at 10) into metric trajectories and calibration quantities (\cref{alg:dtx}).

\paragraph{Stabilisation.} Wind caused slow drift of the hovering drone, so every frame was registered to a reference frame with Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF
(ORB) features~\cite{rublee2011orb}, filtered by Random Sample
Consensus (RANSAC)~\cite{fischler1981ransac}.   The estimated $3\times3$ matrix
$\mathbf{H}_t$ warps frame $t$ onto the reference.

\paragraph{Detection.} Vehicles were detected with YOLOv8s~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}, a single-stage
You Only Look Once (YOLO) detector, initialised from weights trained on the aerial VisDrone2019-DET
dataset~\cite{zhu2022visdrone}, whose \emph{tricycle} and \emph{awning-tricycle} classes resemble Sri
Lankan three-wheelers. The model was fine-tuned on \est{450} annotated frames from the two sessions
with the seven classes of \cref{tab:impl-vtypes}. At about \est{90~m} altitude a motorcycle occupies
roughly 20 pixels, so inference used Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI)~\cite{akyon2022sahi} with
$2\times2$ tiles and an overlap ratio of 0.2.

\paragraph{Tracking.} Detections were linked into tracks with ByteTrack~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack}
(track threshold 0.5, match threshold 0.8, buffer 30 frames).  Its second pass over low-confidence boxes recovers partly hidden motorcycles.
The class of a track was fixed by majority vote over its frames.

\paragraph{Pixel-to-metre conversion.} A ground-plane homography was estimated from at least four
control points (stop-line ends and lane-marking corners, using the 3.5~m lane width and satellite
imagery). A stabilised pixel $(u,v)$ maps to ground coordinates $(x,y)$ by
\begin{equation}
  \lambda \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{G}
  \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},
  \label{eq:impl-homography}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is a scale factor~\cite{hartley2004mvg}. The resulting ground sample distance (GSD)
was \est{$\approx$2.6~cm/px} and the reprojection error on held-out points \est{0.18~m}.

\paragraph{Smoothing and speed.} Positions were smoothed with a Savitzky--Golay filter
\cite{savitzky1964} (window of 11 samples, polynomial order 2), which removes jitter without the lag of a moving average. The speed at sample $k$ is
\begin{equation}
  v_k = \frac{\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_k - \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{k-1} \rVert}{\Delta t},
  \qquad \Delta t = 0.1~\text{s},
  \label{eq:impl-speed}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_k$ is the smoothed position. 

\paragraph{Zoning and movements.} Polygons were drawn for each approach, each exit and each slip
lane, together with stop-line segments. The entry and exit polygons crossed by a track define its
turning movement (left via slip lane, through, right or U-turn).

\paragraph{Waiting time and delay.} As in \sumo{}, a vehicle is waiting in any sample where its speed is
below 0.1~m/s while it is inside an approach zone $\mathcal{Z}$. The waiting time of vehicle $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  W_i = \sum_{k} \Delta t \cdot \mathbb{1}\!\left[ v_k < 0.1~\text{m/s} \;\wedge\; \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_k \in \mathcal{Z} \right],
  \label{eq:impl-wait}
\end{equation}
and its control delay is
\begin{equation}
  d_i = T_{\text{frame},i} - \frac{L_{\text{path},i}}{v_{\text{free}}},
  \label{eq:impl-delay}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{frame},i}$ is the time spent in the field of view, $L_{\text{path},i}$ the length of the
smoothed path and $v_{\text{free}}$ the observed free-flow speed of \est{38~km/h}. Tracks that were
already present when the video began, or still present when it ended, are \emph{censored}: they were counted in flows but excluded from averages.

\paragraph{Derived quantities.} Flows per approach and class were aggregated into 5-minute bins in
vehicles per hour and in Passenger Car Units (PCU) per hour,  with turning ratios and queue lengths. Saturation flow was estimated from queue-discharge headways
at the start of green, ignoring the first three vehicles
\cite{hcm2016}. Expressed per PCU,
\begin{equation}
  s = 3600 \cdot \frac{\sum_{j\geq4} \text{PCU}_j}{\sum_{j\geq4} h_j},
  \label{eq:impl-satflow}
\end{equation}
where $h_j$ is the stop-line headway of the $j$-th discharging vehicle. Pooled over both sessions this
gave \est{1850~PCU/h/lane}.

\paragraph{Output.} Finally, \dtx{} wrote a \sumo{} route file (a vehicle-type distribution per session
and one \texttt{<flow>} per movement per 5-minute bin) and a comma-separated values (CSV) file of
observed key performance indicators (KPIs) for calibration. In the morning the observed demand
was \est{3,420~veh/h}, or \est{3,150~PCU/h}, and in the afternoon it was \est{2,280~veh/h}, or
\est{2,085~PCU/h}. The observed average waiting times under the existing fixed-time plan were
\est{104.7~s} and \est{46.3~s} respectively.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{video $V$ (30 fps), zone polygons $\mathcal{Z}$, control points, model weights}
\KwOut{trajectory table $\mathcal{T}$, route file \texttt{.rou.xml}, observed-KPI CSV}
$F_0 \gets$ reference frame; $\mathcal{T} \gets \emptyset$\;
\ForEach{frame $F_t$ of $V$ sampled at 10 fps}{
  $\mathbf{H}_t \gets$ \textsc{RANSAC}(\textsc{ORBMatch}$(F_t, F_0)$); $\hat F_t \gets$ \textsc{Warp}$(F_t, \mathbf{H}_t)$\;
  $D_t \gets$ \textsc{SAHI}(YOLOv8s, $\hat F_t$, tiles $2\times2$, overlap 0.2)\;
  $\mathcal{T} \gets$ \textsc{ByteTrack}$(\mathcal{T}, D_t)$\;
}
\ForEach{track $\tau \in \mathcal{T}$}{
  class$(\tau) \gets$ majority vote of per-frame classes\;
  map pixels to metres with $\mathbf{G}$ (\cref{eq:impl-homography}); smooth (Savitzky--Golay 11/2)\;
  compute $v_k$ (\cref{eq:impl-speed}), $W_\tau$ (\cref{eq:impl-wait}) and $d_\tau$ (\cref{eq:impl-delay})\;
  movement$(\tau) \gets$ (entry zone, exit zone)\;
  \lIf{$\tau$ touches the video start or end}{mark $\tau$ censored}
}
aggregate flows (veh/h, PCU/h) per approach, class and 5-min bin; turning ratios; queues\;
$s \gets$ \cref{eq:impl-satflow} over discharge headways of 4th and later vehicles\;
write \texttt{vTypeDistribution} and \texttt{<flow>} elements; write KPI CSV (uncensored averages)\;
\caption{\dtx{} drone trajectory extraction}\label{alg:dtx}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{SUMO network and demand}\label{sec:impl-sumo}

The network was taken from OpenStreetMap (OSM)~\cite{haklay2008osm} as an Overpass extract covering the two
roads and the four slip links within 500~m of \ang{6.80133} N, \ang{79.93502} E, and converted with
\texttt{netconvert --lefthand} because Sri Lanka drives on the left. The geometry needed correcting in several
places, but the corrections were \emph{not} made by hand in \texttt{netedit}: each one is recorded as a
\texttt{netconvert} patch file (edge type overrides, connection definitions and the traffic-light program), so
a single script rebuilds the network identically from the extract. This matters for reproducibility (NFR05),
because a hand-edited \texttt{.net.xml} cannot be regenerated once the source data change. All side streets in
the extract were deleted; only the bypass, the cross road and the four slip links are modelled, since the side
streets carry negligible flow and are not signalised. The four arms are numbered clockwise from the northern
bypass arm as Bypass~1 (B1), Cross~1 (X1), Bypass~2 (B2) and Cross~2 (X2); Bypass~1 is therefore the northern
bypass arm, and this numbering is used throughout the thesis and in every output file.

Each arm has two entry and two exit lanes; the kerb lane carries through traffic
and the offside lane carries through and right-turning traffic. Each corner's channelised left-turn
slip lane was modelled as a separate edge that merges into the exit arm through an unsignalised
\emph{yield} link. The slip lanes are therefore not part of the Traffic Light System (TLS) program and
are ignored by the controller except as downstream exit occupancy. The central node is a
\texttt{traffic\_light} junction with the custom four-phase program of \cref{app:phases}: $\phi_1$
bypass through, $\phi_2$ bypass protected right, $\phi_3$ cross-road through and $\phi_4$ cross-road
protected right, each serving both opposing directions and each followed by 3~s amber and 2~s all-red.

Heterogeneous traffic was represented with the sublane model~\cite{sumo_sublane} at a lateral
resolution of 0.8~m. Motorcycles were given a high \texttt{lcSublane} eagerness and
\texttt{minGapLat} of 0.3~m so that they filter between queued cars to the stop line, which is the
dominant behaviour at Maviththara. Longitudinal motion used the Krauss model~\cite{krauss1998} with
driver imperfection $\sigma = 0.5$ and reaction time $\tau = 1.0$~s, and lateral decisions used the
model of Erdmann~\cite{erdmann2015lanechange}. A custom \texttt{threewheeler} type was created because
\sumo{} has no such class. The vehicle types are listed in \cref{tab:impl-vtypes}; the PCU values are
adopted values for urban mixed traffic~\cite{irc106}.

The \emph{height} column was set explicitly rather than left at \sumo{}'s defaults, because the occlusion rule
of the \vls{} (\cref{sec:impl-vls}) treats any vehicle at least 2.5~m tall as an occluder, on the reasoning that
only buses and lorries block the sensor's line of sight. \sumo{}'s defaults contradict that rule: a
\texttt{delivery} van defaults to 2.86~m and a \texttt{truck} to 2.4~m, so with the defaults every van would
occlude and no lorry would. The values in \cref{tab:impl-vtypes} are typical kerb heights for the Sri Lankan
fleet and place exactly the buses and lorries above the threshold.

Demand for every experiment is written by a \emph{scenario generator} parameterised from the \dtx{} outputs.
Two scripts make up the generator. \texttt{sumo/gen\_mock\_routes.py} writes the three balanced scenarios, with
equal demand on every approach at 300, 500 and 700~veh/h; these are the scenarios on which the components were
developed, unit-tested and tuned. \texttt{sumo/gen\_scenario\_routes.py} writes the seven situations that the
drone survey showed make the fixed plan inefficient, listed in \cref{tab:impl-scenarios}. Every scenario shares
the turning shares of 25\% left, 50\% through and 25\% right and the composition of 40\% motorcycles, 20\%
three-wheelers, 25\% cars, 7\% vans, 5\% buses and 3\% lorries, which averages 0.905~PCU per vehicle, and in
every scenario vehicles are released as a Poisson process at the stated rate, so that the ten seeds of a
scenario differ in arrival realisation but not in expected demand. Shares and composition come from the
footage; the ten scenarios are therefore demand conditions observed at or derived from the junction, and the
generator exists so that each condition can be replayed identically under every controller.

Simulations used a step length of 0.5~s, a controller decision every 1~s, a 300~s
warm-up that was discarded and 3,600~s of measurement, with tuning seeds 1--5 and evaluation seeds
6--15. Trip information, summary, queue and emission outputs (HBEFA4~\cite{hbefa41}) were recorded.
The calibration of \texttt{sigma}, \texttt{tau}, \texttt{minGap}, \texttt{lcSublane} and the speed
factor distribution against the observed counts and waiting times is reported in
\cref{sec:eval-calibration}.

\paragraph{Software environment.} \sumo{} \est{1.20.0} is run inside an Ubuntu 24.04 virtual machine hosted by
OrbStack on the author's Mac, rather than natively, because the published macOS binaries no longer run on the
current version of macOS. The virtual machine also fixes the \sumo{} build for the whole experiment campaign,
which removes a source of irreproducibility between runs made weeks apart.

\subsection{Virtual LiDAR Sensor (VLS)}\label{sec:impl-vls}

The \vls{} reproduces the output of a 360\textdegree{} unit,
comparable to a 32-channel Ouster OS1-32~\cite{ouster_os1}, mounted on a 6~m pole at the junction centre. Rather than simulating individual laser returns, \vls{} works at the \emph{object-list} level produced by roadside LiDAR perception software~\cite{zhao2019lidar,lv2019lidar}. Every second it queries all vehicles
through the Traffic Control Interface (TraCI)~\cite{wegener2008traci}, keeps those within
$R = 75$~m and applies the error model below (\cref{alg:vls}).

Each vehicle is represented by the centre of its bounding rectangle. This conversion is necessary because
TraCI reports a vehicle's position at the \emph{front bumper}, whereas a LiDAR perception stack reports the
centroid of the cluster it detects; using the bumper position unchanged would displace every object forward by
half its length, which is 5.75~m for a bus and would corrupt both the range and the distance to the stop line.
The rectangle is also what gives the object its angular extent: the azimuth span $[\alpha_i^-,\alpha_i^+]$ used
in \cref{eq:impl-occlusion} is the smallest interval containing the bearings of the rectangle's four corners,
so a bus seen broadside occludes a wide arc and the same bus seen end-on occludes a narrow one.

Position noise grows with range $r$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{p} + \boldsymbol{\eta}, \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\eta} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0}, \sigma(r)^2\mathbf{I}\right), \qquad
  \sigma(r) = 0.10 + 0.002\,r~\text{m}.
  \label{eq:impl-noise}
\end{equation}
Each object is independently missed with probability
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{miss}}(r) = 0.01 + 0.04\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2},
  \label{eq:impl-dropout}
\end{equation}
which reflects the thinning of beams with distance. Occlusion is computed by ray casting in the
horizontal plane. Let the interval $[\alpha_i^-, \alpha_i^+]$ be the azimuth range that object $i$
subtends at the sensor. Object $i$ is hidden if a nearer object $j$ that is at least 2.5~m tall
(a bus or lorry) satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\big|[\alpha_i^-, \alpha_i^+] \cap [\alpha_j^-, \alpha_j^+]\big|}{\alpha_i^+ - \alpha_i^-} \;\geq\; 0.7 .
  \label{eq:impl-occlusion}
\end{equation}
 Clutter is
added as $N_c \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda = 0.2)$ false objects per scan at uniformly random positions
within range.

A real LiDAR does not know vehicle classes, so \vls{} reports only a \emph{size class} derived from the
measured length: \emph{small} below 3.2~m (motorcycles, bicycles and three-wheelers) mapped to
0.6~PCU, \emph{medium} from 3.2 to 6~m (cars and vans) mapped to 1.0~PCU, and \emph{large} above
6~m (buses and lorries) mapped to 2.8~PCU.  The small and large values are demand-weighted compromises between the classes they contain. The controller never sees the true class.

Raw detections then pass through a perception tracker that associates each detection with the nearest
predicted track within a 2.5~m gate and filters it with an $\alpha$--$\beta$ filter:
\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{-}_{k} &= \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{k-1} + \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\,\Delta t, \label{eq:impl-ab1}\\
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{k} &= \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{-}_{k} + \alpha\,(\mathbf{z}_k - \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{-}_{k}), \label{eq:impl-ab2}\\
  \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k} &= \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1} + \frac{\beta}{\Delta t}\,(\mathbf{z}_k - \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{-}_{k}), \label{eq:impl-ab3}
\end{align}
with $\alpha = 0.5$, $\beta = 0.1$ and $\Delta t = 1$~s.

\paragraph{Track initiation.} A new detection starts a \emph{tentative} track with unknown velocity. Its
second detection is associated using a wider \emph{initiation gate} of $v_{\max}\Delta t \approx 20$~m rather
than the steady-state 2.5~m gate, and the two positions set the initial velocity directly,
$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = (\mathbf{z}_2 - \mathbf{z}_1)/\Delta t$. Only from the third scan onwards does the track
use the 2.5~m gate and the $\alpha$--$\beta$ filter of
\cref{eq:impl-ab1,eq:impl-ab2,eq:impl-ab3}. The wider gate is not a detail. A tentative track initialised with
$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{0}$ predicts that the vehicle has not moved, so with a 2.5~m gate and a 1~s scan
period no vehicle travelling faster than 2.5~m/s, or 9~km/h, can ever be associated with its own second
detection and confirmed. Measured on the balanced 500~veh/h scenario, the original single-gate scheme
confirmed only \est{21\%} of moving vehicles; two-point initiation removes the speed ceiling, because the
gate now spans the furthest a vehicle could legally travel between scans. A track is deleted after three
consecutive misses, which suppresses most single-scan clutter.

\paragraph{Coasting tracks.} Only tracks that were updated by a detection in the current scan are reported to
the controller. A track that was not associated this scan is still propagated internally by
\cref{eq:impl-ab1}, so that it survives a short dropout and can be re-associated, but it is withheld from the
object list. Reporting coasting tracks was found to be actively harmful: they made up \est{30\%} of the object
list, and \est{84\%} of those were more than 2.5~m from any real vehicle, so the controller was counting
queue that had already discharged. Withholding them costs nothing, because a vehicle missed in one scan is
almost always detected in the next and rejoins the list with its position corrected.

\paragraph{Map matching.} Reported tracks are matched to the network by point-in-polygon tests against lane
polygons. A track inside an approach-lane polygon within 75~m of the stop line yields an approach, a lane and
a distance to the stop line measured along the road. A track inside an exit-lane polygon yields the exit link
it occupies, which feeds the downstream occupancy term. Tracks in the junction box itself, on the unsignalised
slip lanes, or further than 75~m along an arm match nothing and are discarded, since the controller has no
state in which to place them.

One feature of the OSM geometry had to be handled explicitly. OSM splits every arm at an intermediate node 8
to 17~m short of the stop line, where the carriageway widens for the slip lane. \sumo{} inserts an internal
lane across that node, and an internal lane belongs to no edge, so a vehicle standing on it matched no
approach polygon at all. The lane polygons therefore include a \emph{connector} spanning the internal lane,
and distance along the road is measured through the connector as well as the two edges it joins. Without this,
the controller saw only \est{27--31\%} of the stopped vehicles on the B1 and X1 approaches, precisely the
vehicles nearest the stop line and so precisely those whose queue matters most.

\paragraph{Effective queue range.} The 75~m radius is centred on the sensor at the junction centre, not on the
stop lines, and the stop lines lie 13 to 16~m from the centre. The sensor therefore observes queues to roughly
\textbf{60~m} back from the stop line, not 75~m. Two statements elsewhere in the original specification assumed
the larger figure and are corrected accordingly: the queue term $Q_m$ of \cref{sec:impl-prism} counts objects
within the \emph{observable} range rather than ``within 75~m of the stop line'', and the exit capacity
$C_b = 2 \times 75/6.5 \approx 23$~PCU is similarly optimistic, since the exit links are observable over the
same shorter distance. This is the limitation that motivates the hidden-queue state of \cref{sec:impl-prism}.

\paragraph{Randomness.} The \vls{} draws its noise, dropout and clutter from its own random generator, seeded
with the run seed and separate from \sumo{}'s, so that sensor realisations are reproducible and independent of
how many random numbers the traffic model happens to consume. For the dropout sweep of
\cref{sec:eval-sensitivity} the range-dependent $p_{\text{miss}}(r)$ of \cref{eq:impl-dropout} is replaced by a
uniform miss probability applied to every object regardless of range, so that the swept quantity is a single
interpretable number.

This list is the only input to \prism{}, so a real sensor could replace it unchanged.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{ground-truth vehicles $\mathcal{V}$ from TraCI, perception tracks $\mathcal{K}$}
\KwOut{object list $\mathcal{O}$ for the controller}
$\mathbf{p}_i \gets$ rectangle centre of $i$ (TraCI reports the front bumper)\;
$\mathcal{D} \gets \{ i \in \mathcal{V} : r_i \leq R \}$\;
\ForEach{$i \in \mathcal{D}$ in increasing range}{
  $[\alpha_i^-,\alpha_i^+] \gets$ azimuth span of the four corners of $i$'s rectangle\;
  \lIf{$\exists j : h_j \geq 2.5~\text{m},\, r_j < r_i$, overlap $\geq 0.7$ (\cref{eq:impl-occlusion})}{remove $i$; \textbf{continue}}
  \lIf{$U(0,1) < p_{\text{miss}}(r_i)$}{remove $i$; \textbf{continue}}
  $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_i \gets \mathbf{p}_i + \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma(r_i)^2\mathbf{I})$; size class and PCU from length\;
}
add $N_c \sim \text{Poisson}(0.2)$ clutter objects uniformly within $R$\;
\ForEach{tentative track with one hit}{associate within the initiation gate $v_{\max}\Delta t \approx 20$~m; set $\hat{\mathbf{v}} \gets (\mathbf{z}_2 - \mathbf{z}_1)/\Delta t$}
associate remaining detections to $\mathcal{K}$ by nearest neighbour (gate 2.5~m); update with \cref{eq:impl-ab1,eq:impl-ab2,eq:impl-ab3}\;
coast unassociated tracks by \cref{eq:impl-ab1} but do \emph{not} report them; delete after 3 misses\;
$\mathcal{O} \gets$ tracks updated this scan, map-matched to approach, lane and $d_{\text{stop}}$ through the intermediate-node connectors; junction box, slip lanes and anything beyond 75~m match nothing\;
\caption{\vls{} scan and perception}\label{alg:vls}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{PRISM control algorithm (PRISM-R)}\label{sec:impl-prism}

\prism{} belongs to the max-pressure family~\cite{varaiya2013maxpressure,tassiulas1992} but was adapted
to three realities of the Maviththara junction: vehicles differ greatly in size, a starved approach is
socially unacceptable even if it is theoretically optimal, and the sensor is imperfect.  It contains no learned component.

The law as first specified did not work. On its first evaluation it lost to the existing fixed-time plan at
both 500 and 700~veh/h, and the loss at 700 was severe. Rather than retune the parameters, the author
instrumented the decision log and diagnosed five distinct causes, each of which is a modelling error rather
than a bad constant:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The map-matching gap.} Vehicles standing on the internal lane at the intermediate OSM node,
        nearest the stop line, matched no approach, so the controller saw only \est{27--31\%} of the stopped
        vehicles on B1 and X1 (\cref{sec:impl-vls}). It was optimising against a queue that was mostly
        invisible.
  \item \textbf{$G_{\text{req}}$ recomputed every second.} \Cref{eq:greq} was evaluated at each decision step
        from the queue \emph{currently} present. As the green discharged the queue, $G_{\text{req}}$ shrank
        with it, so the condition $t_g \geq \rho\,G_{\text{req}}$ became satisfiable almost immediately. Greens
        ended after about \est{11~s} and the controller made roughly \est{250} phase changes an hour, each
        paying 5~s of intergreen: about 21~minutes of every hour was spent clearing the junction.
  \item \textbf{The 60~m horizon.} Because the sensor sees roughly 60~m back from the stop line, a queue that
        is longer than that reports a constant value, and the controller cannot distinguish a queue of 60~m
        from one of 250~m. At 700~veh/h this is the normal condition, so the pressure signal saturated exactly
        when it was needed.
  \item \textbf{Right-turn starvation.} Under acyclic arg-max selection a protected right turn, which serves
        at most a quarter of the flow of the through phase beside it, is almost never the strongest phase. It
        was served only when the starvation override fired at 120~s, and the resulting right-turn queues then
        blocked the offside lane and degraded the through movement as well.
  \item \textbf{Occlusion in heavy traffic.} A stopped vehicle hidden behind a bus simply vanished from the
        object list, so its PCU left the queue estimate although the vehicle had not moved.
\end{enumerate}

The revisions described in the remainder of this section address these five causes. Every revision is
implemented as a parameter rather than as a rewrite, so the original formulation can still be executed on
identical sensing as an ablation, referred to throughout \cref{ch:eval} as ``\prism{}, thesis law''. The
revised controller is denoted \prism{}-R, and wherever the two differ the text says which is meant.

\subsubsection{State}

A \emph{movement} $m$ is a pair (approach, through or right). Left turns use the slip lanes and are
not controlled. With the lane assumption stated in \cref{sec:impl-sumo}, the controller computes for
each movement:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $Q_m$, the queued PCU: the sum of PCU of objects in $m$'s lanes with $v < 1.4$~m/s within the
        observable range of the approach, which is about 60~m from the stop line (\cref{sec:impl-vls}).
        Offside-lane objects are split between through and right, because the sensor cannot know a stationary
        vehicle's intention;
  \item $A_m$, the arriving PCU: the sum of PCU of moving objects whose estimated time of
        arrival (ETA) $d_{\text{stop}}/v$ is at most $H = 10$~s;
  \item $O_b$, the PCU occupancy of exit link $b$, whose nominal capacity is $C_b \approx 23$~PCU
        ($2 \times 75~\text{m} / 6.5~\text{m}$ per PCU). This figure shares the optimism noted in
        \cref{sec:impl-vls}, since the exit is observable over roughly 60~m rather than 75~m; it is retained
        because the spillback guard compares a ratio against a fixed threshold, so a consistently scaled
        capacity shifts only where that threshold sits.
\end{itemize}
For each phase $\phi$, $R_\phi$ is the time since $\phi$ last had green. \prism{}-R adds three further state
elements.

\paragraph{Offside-lane split.} The offside lane carries both through and right-turning traffic, and the
kerb lane carries only through traffic. Splitting an offside-lane object by the raw turning shares is
therefore wrong, because it ignores the fact that part of the through traffic is not in that lane at all. If
$T$ and $R$ are the through and right turning shares and a fraction $f_k = $ \est{0.75} of through traffic uses
the kerb lane, the share of offside-lane traffic that is turning right is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_R = \frac{R}{R + (1 - f_k)\,T},
  \label{eq:impl-offside}
\end{equation}
which for the observed shares gives \est{0.667} rather than the 0.33 that the raw shares would suggest. A
stationary offside-lane object contributes $\pi_R$ of its PCU to the right movement and $1-\pi_R$ to the
through movement.

\paragraph{Hidden queue.} While a movement's visible queue reaches the edge of the sensor range, that is,
while some stopped object of the movement lies at least 50~m back, the controller can no longer measure the
queue and must instead account for it. Arrivals at the learnt rate $\lambda_m$ are accumulated into a
\emph{hidden queue} $Q^{\text{hid}}_m$, where $\lambda_m$ is an exponentially weighted moving average, with
time constant $\tau_\lambda = 120$~s, of the rate at which tracks enter the range beyond 45~m. A track that
enters the observable range is removed from the hidden queue, so the same vehicle is never counted twice. The
hidden queue is capped at 60~PCU, which is roughly the standing capacity of an approach beyond the sensor, and
is cleared entirely whenever every object of the movement is nearer than 30~m, since the queue has then
demonstrably dissipated. It is added to $Q_m$ in proportion to $T$ and $R$. This is the device discussed in
\cref{sec:lit-algorithms}: it makes the state degrade conservatively, rather than silently, at the range edge.

\paragraph{Occlusion persistence.} A stopped object on an approach that disappears from the object list
without ever having been seen moving is held in the state, at its last position, for a further 5~s. A vehicle
that stops behind a bus does not cease to occupy road space, and the alternative, deleting it immediately, was
what allowed heavy-vehicle traffic to erase its own queue from the estimate. Five seconds is short enough that
a vehicle which genuinely departed during an occlusion is forgotten within one or two decisions.

\subsubsection{Equations}

The movement pressure is
\begin{equation}
  p_m = Q_m + \gamma A_m - \delta\, O_{\text{exit}(m)}, \qquad \gamma = 0.5,\; \delta = 0.6.
  \label{eq:pressure}
\end{equation}
The terms are the upstream queue, the platoon about to arrive and the downstream penalty that discourages feeding a congested exit
\cite{gregoire2015capacity}. Phase pressure aggregates its movements:
\begin{equation}
  P_\phi = \sum_{m \in \phi} p_m .
  \label{eq:phase-pressure}
\end{equation}
To encode fairness, each phase receives an urgency multiplier that grows with the square of its red
time,
\begin{equation}
  U_\phi = 1 + \kappa \left( \frac{R_\phi}{T_{\text{starve}}} \right)^{2}, \qquad \kappa = 1.5,
  \label{eq:urgency}
\end{equation}
so that urgency is negligible for short reds but reaches $1+\kappa = 2.5$ at the starvation cap. The
phase score is
\begin{equation}
  S_\phi = \max(P_\phi, 0)\cdot U_\phi .
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Clipping at zero stops an empty phase being promoted. The green time required to discharge the current demand is estimated as
\begin{equation}
  G_{\text{req}}(\phi) = \operatorname{clip}\!\left( l_s + h_s \cdot
  \frac{\sum_{m \in \phi} (Q_m + \gamma A_m)}{n_{\text{lanes}}(\phi)},\; G_{\min}(\phi),\; G_{\max}(\phi) \right),
  \label{eq:greq}
\end{equation}
with start-up lost time $l_s = 2.0$~s, saturation headway $h_s = 3600/1850 \approx 1.95$~s/PCU and
$n_{\text{lanes}}(\phi)$ the number of approach lanes discharging during $\phi$ (four for a through
phase, two for a right-turn phase).

\Cref{eq:pressure,eq:phase-pressure,eq:urgency,eq:score} are unchanged in \prism{}-R. \Cref{eq:greq} is
amended in two ways, which between them fix causes~2 and~3 of the diagnosis.

\paragraph{Range-edge blend.} If any movement of the phase has a queue that reaches the edge of the sensor
range, the required green cannot be computed from the visible queue, because that queue is a lower bound of
unknown slackness. The estimate is then blended halfway towards the maximum,
\begin{equation}
  G_{\text{req}} \leftarrow G_{\text{req}} + \eta\,\bigl(G_{\max}(\phi) - G_{\text{req}}\bigr),
  \qquad \eta = 0.5,
  \label{eq:impl-edgeblend}
\end{equation}
so that a saturated approach is given roughly the green it would need if the queue continued beyond what can
be seen. Blending rather than jumping to $G_{\max}$ keeps the controller responsive if the queue turns out to
be short.

\paragraph{Frozen target.} $G_{\text{req}}(\phi)$ is evaluated \emph{once}, at the instant the green of
$\phi$ begins, and held constant for the whole of that green. The original specification recomputed it at
every decision step, which made the target chase the shrinking queue it was meant to discharge and produced
the 11~s greens described above. Freezing it restores the intended meaning of the quantity: the green needed
to clear the queue that was present when the green started.

\subsubsection{Decision loop}

Every $\Delta = 1$~s during the green of phase $g$ with elapsed green $t_g$, the controller evaluates
seven rules in order and acts on the first that fires. Say that a phase \emph{can discharge} if it has at
least one queued or arriving PCU on a movement whose exit is not spilling back.
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Minimum green.} If $t_g < G_{\min}(g)$, extend.
  \item \textbf{Spillback or max-out.} If an exit fed by $g$ has $O_b/C_b > 0.85$, terminate. Terminate also
        if $t_g \geq G_{\max}(g)$, but \emph{only} while some other phase is waiting: a green that has reached
        its maximum with nothing else to serve is not worth the 5~s of intergreen it would cost to end.
  \item \textbf{Starvation.} If any other phase $\phi$ has $R_\phi \geq T_{\text{starve}}$ and is waiting,
        terminate. ``Waiting'' means any queued or arriving PCU, $Q_\phi + A_\phi > 0$, rather than the
        original $P_\phi > 0$: a phase whose pressure is driven negative by a congested exit is still waiting,
        and under the original test it could be passed over indefinitely. A phase whose own exit is spilling
        back is \emph{not} forced in by this rule, since releasing traffic into a blocked exit helps no one.
  \item \textbf{Rest in red.} If no approach anywhere at the junction is occupied, terminate and rest in
        all-red. The first vehicle subsequently detected on any arm receives its green directly from all-red,
        so it waits only the clearance interval instead of finding the green committed to an empty approach.
  \item \textbf{Rest in green.} If no other phase can discharge, extend. There is nothing to be gained by
        taking the green away from the only movement able to use it.
  \item \textbf{Gap-out.} If $\sum_{m\in g} Q_m \leq 0.5$~PCU and no object of $g$ has ETA
        $\leq \tau_{\text{gap}} = 3$~s, terminate.
  \item \textbf{Pressure switch.} If $\max_{\phi \neq g} S_\phi > (1+\theta) S_g + \varepsilon$ for a phase
        that can discharge, with $\theta = 0.10$ and $\varepsilon = 2$~PCU, and $t_g \geq \rho\,
        G_{\text{req}}(g)$ with $\rho = 1.0$ evaluated against the frozen target, terminate.
\end{enumerate}
If no rule fires, the green is extended by 1~s.

On termination, 3~s amber and 2~s all-red follow. At the end of the all-red the next phase is chosen. Starved
phases are served first, oldest first. Otherwise selection is \emph{cyclic with skipping}: the controller
takes the first phase after $g$ in the fixed order $\phi_1 \rightarrow \phi_2 \rightarrow \phi_3 \rightarrow
\phi_4$ that can discharge, passing over any phase with nothing to serve. If no phase can discharge, the
strongest phase by $S_\phi$ is chosen. This replaces the acyclic arg-max of the original specification and
follows the cyclic max-pressure construction of Levin, Hu and
Odell~\cite{levin2020cyclic,le2015decentralized}: it guarantees a protected right turn its turn whenever it
has demand, which the arg-max never did, while the skip rule prevents the fixed order from degenerating into
a fixed-time plan.

Two further behaviours, left unspecified in the original design, were settled during the revision. A
\emph{short sensor gap} of one to four scans holds the current green rather than taking any decision, because
a single missed scan is far more likely than a junction that has emptied instantaneously. Entry to the
\emph{fail-safe} is not instantaneous either: the controller runs the normal 3~s amber and 2~s all-red before
handing over, and the fixed plan then starts at the beginning of its $\phi_1$, so that drivers see a lawful
transition and a familiar plan from its natural start. Recovery is likewise deferred until B1 is again at the
start of $\phi_1$, which prevents the adaptive law from resuming mid-cycle.

Two guards complete the design. The \emph{spillback guard} applies when any exit has
$O_b/C_b > 0.85$: every phase feeding $b$ has its effective maximum green reduced to $G_{\min}$, keeping vehicles out of a blocked junction box. The \emph{fail-safe} switches to the observed 78~s fixed-time plan
(B1) when the \vls{} delivers no data for five consecutive scans, and \prism{} resumes at the next
cycle boundary once ten consecutive valid scans have been received.  The complete logic is given in \cref{alg:prism} and
\cref{fig:impl-prism-flow}.

\subsubsection{Properties}

\paragraph{Safety.} \prism{} never writes a signal state directly. It selects one of four
pre-verified green states, and every transition passes through the 3~s amber and 2~s all-red
states of \cref{app:phases}, so conflicting greens and truncated intergreens are impossible by
construction. This was also checked by an automated test over every simulated step
(\cref{app:code}).

\paragraph{Bounded fairness.} Let phase $\phi$ end its green at time $t_0$. It becomes starved at
$t_0 + T_{\text{starve}}$. From then on, rule~3 terminates any running green at the first decision
step after that green reaches $G_{\min}$, and the oldest-first rule serves $\phi$ after the following
5~s intergreen. If another phase became starved earlier, it is served first, but its green is itself
cut at $G_{\min}$ because $\phi$ is starved. The extra time beyond $T_{\text{starve}}$ therefore
consists of at most minimum greens and intergreens, which together are always shorter than one
maximum through green plus one intergreen.

The bound survives the revisions of \prism{}-R because the starvation rule still outranks both resting rules
and the cyclic order: rest in green (rule~5) and the cyclic selection are consulted only after rule~3 has
found no starved phase, so neither can hold the green against a phase that has reached $T_{\text{starve}}$.
The corrected definition of ``waiting'' strengthens the guarantee rather than weakening it, since a phase now
qualifies on $Q_\phi + A_\phi > 0$ and no longer loses its claim merely because a congested exit has driven
its pressure negative. The single exception is deliberate: a phase whose own exit is spilling back is not
forced in, because its vehicles could not discharge in any case, and it reclaims its turn as soon as the exit
clears. A conservative bound on the red time of any phase is
\begin{equation}
  R^{\text{worst}} \leq T_{\text{starve}} + G_{\max} + 5~\text{s} = 120 + 50 + 5 = 175~\text{s},
  \label{eq:impl-fairness}
\end{equation}
using the through-phase $G_{\max}$ of 50~s, which is the longest green any phase can hold. 

\paragraph{Stability intuition.} Varaiya~\cite{varaiya2013maxpressure} proved that serving the phase
of maximum pressure stabilises queues for any demand inside the capacity region, using only local
information. \prism{} keeps that core because phases are ranked by pressure. However, the minimum and
maximum greens, the hysteresis $\theta$ and the urgency factor mean that the formal proof does not
transfer exactly.  This trade-off for fairness is also made by cycle-based variants~\cite{kouvelas2014maxpressure}.
\prism{}-R's cyclic selection makes the position stronger rather than weaker. Levin, Hu and
Odell~\cite{levin2020cyclic} prove that imposing a fixed phase order, with skipping of phases that have no
demand, preserves the maximum stability region of acyclic max-pressure, and Le et
al.~\cite{le2015decentralized} reach a comparable conclusion for constrained decentralised policies. The
cyclic-with-skip rule used here is exactly that construction, so the departure from Varaiya's policy now lies
only in the green bounds, the hysteresis and the urgency term.

\paragraph{Complexity.} Each object is assigned to one movement and one exit, so state computation is
$O(n)$ in the number of objects $n$, and the memory held is likewise $O(n)$: one record per track, plus the
track identifiers and occlusion-held objects, which are retained for 5~s. The mean decision time measured was
\est{0.25~ms} at \est{400} objects, with about \est{162~KiB} retained
(\cref{sec:eval-nonfunctional}); this supersedes the \est{0.4~ms} reported for the original formulation.

\paragraph{Explainability.} Every decision writes a record containing time, phase, $t_g$, the rule
that fired, all $P_\phi$, $U_\phi$ and $S_\phi$, and $G_{\text{req}}$.  For example, ``pressure switch: $S_{\phi_3} = 18.4 > 1.25 \times
11.2 + 2$''. 

\paragraph{Parameter justification.} \Cref{tab:impl-prism-params} lists the design parameters and the
reasoning behind each value. They were fixed on the balanced 300, 500 and 700~veh/h scenarios using tuning
seeds 1--5 only, by a grid search over the edge blend $\eta$, the discharge ratio $\rho$ and the hysteresis
$\theta$; no evaluation seed and none of the seven situation scenarios took part in the tuning.

$l_s$ (start-up lost time) & 2.0 s & Typical start-up lost time~\cite{hcm2016}.\\
$h_s$ (headway per PCU) & 1.95 s & $3600/1850$, from the \dtx{} saturation flow.\\
$G_{\min}$ & 10 / 5 s & Through / right; driver expectation and clearance of the queue head.\\
$G_{\max}$ & 50 / 25 s & Through / right; limits red time of the other three phases.\\
$T_{\text{starve}}$ & 120 s & Longer than any observed red (67 s) and acceptable to waiting drivers.\\
Amber / all-red & 3 s / 2 s & Same as the existing signal.\\
$\Delta$ (decision period) & 1 s & Two \sumo{} steps; matches the \vls{} scan rate.\\
\end{longtable}

\paragraph{B2: SUMO actuated.} \sumo{}'s built-in gap-based actuated controller~\cite{sumo_actuated}
was run on the same four phases with \texttt{minDur} 10/5~s and \texttt{maxDur} 50/25~s for
through/right phases, its \emph{default} induction loops and a maximum gap of 3~s. It extends a green while
vehicles keep arriving but ignores vehicle size and the demand on other phases. One limitation of this
configuration must be stated plainly, because it affects the comparison. \sumo{} generates default detectors
for lanes that serve a phase's \emph{major} movements, and the protected right-turn phases $\phi_2$ and
$\phi_4$ receive no detectors of their own. Those phases therefore cannot gap out on their own demand and
always run to \texttt{minDur}, which starves right turns under heavy demand and is the main reason B2
deteriorates so sharply at 700~veh/h (\cref{sec:eval-results}). Adding hand-placed detectors would have made
B2 stronger, but it would also have made it a different, hand-tuned controller; the default configuration was
kept because it is what an authority deploying \sumo{}-style actuation out of the box would obtain, and the
weakness is reported rather than concealed.

\paragraph{B3: Webster-optimised fixed time.}  This separates adaptivity from simple re-timing. Webster's
method~\cite{webster1958} was applied \emph{per scenario}, to the mean demand that
\texttt{gen\_scenario\_routes.py} writes for that scenario, so that each scenario is compared against the best
fixed plan for its own demand rather than against one plan for all ten.
Left turns use the slip lanes and were excluded. Because waiting right-turners occupy the offside lane
during through phases, \dtx{} showed that \est{75\%} of through traffic used the kerb lane, so the
critical lane flow of a through phase is $0.75$ of the heavier approach's through flow. For a
right-turn phase it is the full right-turn flow in the offside lane. The flow ratio of phase $i$ is
$y_i = q_i/s$ with $s = $ \est{1850~PCU/h/lane}, $Y = \sum_i y_i$, the lost time is
$L = 4 \times 5 = 20$~s, and
\begin{equation}
  C_0 = \frac{1.5L + 5}{1 - Y}, \qquad g_i = (C_0 - L)\,\frac{y_i}{Y}.
  \label{eq:impl-webster}
\end{equation}
At the demand levels of most scenarios $Y$ is small, so \cref{eq:impl-webster} returns a cycle whose effective
green $C_0 - L$ is shorter than the sum of the minimum greens, $\sum_i G_{\min}(i) = 10+5+10+5 = 30$~s. In
that case the cycle is raised to $\sum_i G_{\min}(i) + L$ and the greens are set to the minima, since a plan
that cannot clear the head of a queue is not a fair benchmark. The result is a 50~s cycle at 10/5/10/5~s for
most scenarios, and a 61~s cycle at 13/8/12/8~s for the heavy-vehicle scenario and for balanced 700~veh/h,
where $Y$ is large enough for \cref{eq:impl-webster} to bind (\cref{tab:impl-webster}).

Two observations follow. First, in eight of the ten scenarios Webster's formula asks for less green than the
minimum greens allow, so B3 reduces to the shortest lawful cycle. This is not a defect of the method but a
consequence of the junction being undersaturated for much of the day, and it already represents a large
improvement on the existing plan, whose 78~s cycle spends 20~s of every cycle on intergreens regardless of
demand. Second, where the formula does bind, at balanced 700~veh/h and in the heavy-vehicle scenario, it
lengthens the cycle to 61~s and shifts green towards the bypass. The comparison with the existing plan is
instructive in its own right: under B1, $\phi_1$ carries a degree of saturation
$x = y\,C/g$ well above unity at the observed morning demand, so the bypass through movement is heavily
oversaturated while $\phi_4$ runs far below capacity. The existing plan therefore allocates its green time
to the wrong phases and uses one cycle length for very different demand levels.
However, Webster's formula assumes stationary random arrivals and undersaturation, and it ignores
queues that spill back past the slip-lane entries, so it cannot respond to the peak, the surge or the empty
side road at all. The simulated benefit of B3 was therefore expected
to be smaller than the analytical one (\cref{sec:eval-results}).

\paragraph{PRISM-GT (oracle).} The same \prism{}-R code was run with the \vls{} replaced by an exact
ground-truth object list, with true positions, no dropout or occlusion and true-class PCU rather than
size-class PCU. The gap between \prism{}-R and PRISM-GT measures the cost of imperfect sensing.

\paragraph{PRISM, thesis law (ablation).} The control law exactly as first specified in this chapter, before
the revisions of \cref{sec:impl-prism}, was run on identical sensing: recomputed $G_{\text{req}}$, no
range-edge blend, no hidden queue, no occlusion persistence, $\theta = 0.25$, $\rho = 0.6$, six rules and
acyclic arg-max selection. Because every revision is a parameter, this ablation differs from \prism{}-R only
in configuration, so the difference between them isolates the effect of the revisions rather than of any
other change. It is reported alongside the baselines throughout \cref{ch:eval}.

The code follows the repository layout planned in \cref{sec:srs-arch}: \texttt{extraction/} for \dtx{},
\texttt{sumo/} for the network and demand, \texttt{sensing/} for the \vls{}, \texttt{control/} for

\paragraph{Waiting-time computation.} \Cref{lst:impl-wait} implements \cref{eq:impl-speed,eq:impl-wait,eq:impl-delay}.
The decisive detail is that the 0.1~m/s threshold is the same constant \sumo{} uses, so observed and
simulated waiting times are directly comparable during calibration. 

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py,caption={Per-vehicle waiting time and control delay (\texttt{extraction/metrics.py})},label={lst:impl-wait}]
WAIT_SPEED = 0.1   # m/s, identical to SUMO's waitingTime threshold
DT = 0.1           # s, video processed at 10 fps

def vehicle_metrics(trk: pd.DataFrame, zones: ZoneMap, v_free: float) -> dict:
    """Waiting time W_i, time in frame and control delay d_i for one track."""
    xy = savgol_filter(trk[["x", "y"]].to_numpy(), 11, 2, axis=0)
    step = np.linalg.norm(np.diff(xy, axis=0), axis=1)
    v = np.r_[step[0], step] / DT                     # speed per sample
    in_approach = zones.in_approach(xy)               # point-in-polygon
    W = DT * np.count_nonzero((v < WAIT_SPEED) & in_approach)
    t_frame = trk.t.iloc[-1] - trk.t.iloc[0]
    delay = max(t_frame - step.sum() / v_free, 0.0)
    censored = (trk.t.iloc[0] <= zones.t_start + DT or
                trk.t.iloc[-1] >= zones.t_end - DT)
    return {"track_id": int(trk.track_id.iloc[0]),
            "cls": trk.cls.mode().iat[0],             # majority vote
            "movement": zones.movement(xy),
            "W": W, "t_frame": t_frame, "delay": delay,
            "censored": censored}
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{VLS scan.} \Cref{lst:impl-vls} shows the error model. Vehicles are processed in increasing
range so that the list of potential occluders only contains nearer tall vehicles, which keeps the
occlusion test cheap.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py,caption={Noisy, occluded LiDAR scan (\texttt{sensing/virtual\_lidar.py})},label={lst:impl-vls}]
def scan(self, vehicles: list[GTVehicle]) -> list[Detection]:
    near = sorted((v for v in vehicles if self.range(v) <= self.R), key=self.range)
    dets, blockers = [], []                   # blockers: tall vehicles seen so far
    for v in near:
        r = self.range(v)
        span = self.azimuth_span(v)
        if any(overlap(span, b) >= 0.7 * width(span) for b in blockers):
            continue                          # occluded by bus/lorry
        if v.height >= 2.5:
            blockers.append(span)
        if self.rng.random() < 0.01 + 0.04 * (r / self.R) ** 2:
            continue                          # range-dependent dropout
        sigma = 0.10 + 0.002 * r
        x, y = v.pos + self.rng.normal(0.0, sigma, 2)
        length = v.length + self.rng.normal(0.0, sigma)
        dets.append(Detection(x, y, size_class(length)))
    for _ in range(self.rng.poisson(0.2)):    # clutter
        dets.append(Detection(*self.random_point_in_range(), "small"))
    return dets

def size_class(length: float) -> str:
    return "small" if length < 3.2 else "medium" if length <= 6.0 else "large"
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Pressure and score.} \Cref{lst:impl-pressure} computes \cref{eq:pressure,eq:phase-pressure,eq:urgency,eq:score}
in a single pass over the object list. This is the source of the $O(n)$ complexity.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py,caption={Movement pressure and phase score (\texttt{control/prism.py})},label={lst:impl-pressure}]
def movement_state(self, objs: list[TrackedObject]) -> dict[Movement, MovState]:
    st = {m: MovState() for m in MOVEMENTS}
    seen = {m: [] for m in MOVEMENTS}
    for o in objs + self.held_occluded(self.t_now):    # single O(n) pass
        if o.approach is None:                         # exit link
            self.occ[o.exit_link] += o.pcu
            continue
        for m, share in self.split(o):                 # offside lane: eq. (5.x)
            if o.v < Q_SPEED and o.d_stop <= self.p.range_m:
                st[m].Q += share * o.pcu
                seen[m].append(o.d_stop)
            elif o.v >= Q_SPEED and o.d_stop / o.v <= self.p.H:
                st[m].A += share * o.pcu
                st[m].min_eta = min(st[m].min_eta, o.d_stop / o.v)
    for m, ds in seen.items():                         # hidden queue at the range edge
        st[m].at_edge = bool(ds) and max(ds) >= self.p.edge_m      # 50 m
        self.hidden[m] = self.update_hidden(m, st[m].at_edge, objs)
        st[m].Q += self.hidden[m]
    return st

def scores(self, st, t_now: float) -> dict[int, float]:
    S = {}
    for ph in PHASES:
        P = sum(st[m].Q + self.p.gamma * st[m].A
                - self.p.delta * self.occ[EXIT[m]] for m in ph.movements)
        U = 1.0 + self.p.kappa * (self.red_time(ph, t_now) / self.p.T_starve) ** 2
        S[ph.id] = max(P, 0.0) * U
    return S
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Decision loop.} \Cref{lst:impl-decide} is a direct transcription of the seven rules. Each branch returns a reason string, which provides the explainability. Note that \texttt{self.g\_req\_frozen} is set once, in \texttt{start\_green()}, and is not recomputed here.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py,caption={The \texttt{decide()} rule cascade (\texttt{control/prism.py})},label={lst:impl-decide}]
def decide(self, objs, t_now: float) -> Decision:
    st = self.movement_state(objs)
    S = self.scores(st, t_now)
    g, tg, p = self.green, t_now - self.green_start, self.p
    others = [ph for ph in PHASES if ph is not g]
    waiting = [ph for ph in others if self.is_waiting(ph, st)]
    ready = [ph for ph in others if self.can_discharge(ph, st)]
    if tg < g.gmin:                                           # R1
        return Decision(EXTEND, "min_green", S)
    if self.spillback(g):                                     # R2
        return Decision(TERMINATE, "spillback", S)
    if tg >= g.gmax and waiting:
        return Decision(TERMINATE, "max_out", S)
    if self.starved(st, t_now, exclude=g):                    # R3
        return Decision(TERMINATE, "starvation", S)
    if not self.any_occupied(st):                             # R4
        return Decision(TERMINATE, "rest_in_red", S)
    if not ready:                                             # R5
        return Decision(EXTEND, "rest_in_green", S)
    queue_g = sum(st[m].Q for m in g.movements)
    eta_g = min(st[m].min_eta for m in g.movements)
    if queue_g <= 0.5 and eta_g > p.tau_gap:                  # R6
        return Decision(TERMINATE, "gap_out", S)
    best = max(S[ph.id] for ph in ready)                      # R7
    if (best > (1 + p.theta) * S[g.id] + p.eps
            and tg >= p.rho * self.g_req_frozen):
        return Decision(TERMINATE, "pressure_switch", S)
    return Decision(EXTEND, "extend", S)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{TraCI integration.} \Cref{lst:impl-loop} shows the experiment loop. The controller is called
every second step, which gives the 1~s decision period with a 0.5~s simulation step. Signal states are
set only through the phase manager, which inserts amber and all-red. The same loop drives B1--B3,
\prism{} and PRISM-GT, selected from \texttt{scenarios.yaml}, so the comparison is like-for-like.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=py,caption={Simulation loop (\texttt{experiments/run\_experiment.py})},label={lst:impl-loop}]
def run(scn: Scenario, seed: int, gui: bool = False) -> Path:
    sim = traci if gui else libsumo
    sim.start(["sumo-gui" if gui else "sumo", "-c", scn.cfg, "--seed", str(seed),
               "--step-length", "0.5", "--lateral-resolution", "0.8",
               "--tripinfo-output", scn.out(seed, "tripinfo.xml")])
    sensor = GroundTruth(sim) if scn.oracle else VirtualLidar(sim, seed=seed)
    tracker = PerceptionTracker(alpha=0.5, beta=0.1, gate=2.5)
    ctrl = make_controller(scn.controller, PhaseManager(sim, TLS_ID))
    log, step = DecisionLog(scn.out(seed, "decisions.csv")), 0
    while sim.simulation.getMinExpectedNumber() > 0 and step < 2 * 3900:
        sim.simulationStep()
        step += 1
        if step % 2:                      # decide every 1 s (two 0.5 s steps)
            continue
        t = sim.simulation.getTime()
        objs = tracker.update(sensor.scan(), t)
        decision = ctrl.step(objs, t)     # PRISM: decide(); B1/B3: plan; B2: SUMO
        log.write(t, decision)
        if gui:
            show_overlay(sim, objs, decision)   # VLS points and reason
    sim.close()
    return scn.out(seed, "tripinfo.xml")

\section{Chapter summary}

This chapter described how the artefact was designed and built. \dtx{} turned the drone videos into
anonymous metric trajectories through stabilisation, YOLOv8s detection with SAHI, ByteTrack tracking,
homography and Savitzky--Golay smoothing, and it measured waiting time with the same 0.1~m/s
definition that \sumo{} uses. The \sumo{} model reproduced the left-hand, 2+2-lane junction with
give-way slip lanes, sublane motorcycles and a four-phase signal, rebuilt reproducibly from the OpenStreetMap
extract by \texttt{netconvert} patches and driven by ten drone-derived demand scenarios. The \vls{} supplied a
noisy, occluded, class-blind object list, with two-point track initiation, coasting tracks withheld and map
matching carried across the intermediate OSM nodes. \prism{} combined PCU-weighted movement pressure, an
arrival term, a downstream penalty and a quadratic urgency factor; after a first evaluation exposed five
distinct modelling errors, it was revised into \prism{}-R, which adds a hidden-queue state, a frozen
green target, a range-edge blend, occlusion persistence, resting in green and in red, and cyclic phase
selection with skipping, in seven transparent rules, with proven safety, a 175~s fairness bound,
$O(n)$ cost and a logged reason for every decision. Three baselines were implemented, including a
Webster plan re-timed per scenario, together with a ground-truth oracle and an ablation that runs the
original law on identical sensing.

\Cref{ch:eval} now tests whether these designs meet the requirements and how \prism{} performs
against the baselines.
