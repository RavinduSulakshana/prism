\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Signalised junctions in the Colombo District are still operated by fixed-time plans that cannot respond to fluctuating, highly heterogeneous demand in which motorcycles and three-wheelers filter between cars, buses and lorries; when queues grow excessive, traffic police switch the signals off and direct traffic manually. This research designed, implemented and evaluated PRISM (Pressure-based Real-time Intersection Signal Management), an adaptive signal controller for the Maviththara junction on the Piliyandala bypass. A drone survey under police, Ministry of Defence and Civil Aviation Authority approval recorded morning-peak and afternoon traffic, and a Drone Trajectory eXtractor combining frame stabilisation, a fine-tuned YOLOv8s detector with sliced inference, ByteTrack tracking and homography-based georeferencing converted the video into vehicle trajectories, demand, turning ratios, saturation flow and waiting times. These data calibrated a sublane SUMO model of the junction. A Virtual LiDAR Sensor reproduced the noise, dropout and occlusion of a pole-mounted LiDAR and supplied the only input to PRISM, whose control law is purely mathematical: a passenger-car-unit weighted max-pressure score with a quadratic starvation-urgency term, hysteresis, gap-out and spillback protection. Over ten held-out random seeds, PRISM reduced average waiting time per vehicle relative to the existing 78-second fixed-time plan by \est{18.8\%} under moderate demand and \est{11.4\%} under heavy demand, outperformed actuated and Webster-optimised control, and lowered the share of cycles that would trigger manual police control from \est{31\%} to \est{19\%}. Performance degraded gracefully under sensor imperfections. The results indicate that privacy-preserving LiDAR sensing combined with a transparent pressure-based control law is a viable path to adaptive signal control at Sri Lankan junctions.
